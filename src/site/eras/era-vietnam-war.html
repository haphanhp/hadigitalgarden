Divided Vietnam and the Vietnam War

1954 to 1975

← Back to the full timeline

The 1954 Geneva Accords ended the First Indochina War but left Vietnam split into two rival states, each backed by opposing sides of the Cold War. What followed was two decades of escalating conflict — first a guerrilla insurgency in the South, then a full-scale war involving half a million American troops, and finally a negotiated American withdrawal followed by the North's final military victory in 1975. Few conflicts of the 20th century remain as contested in their interpretation, and this article tries to lay out the documented sequence of events alongside the major points of historical dispute.

1954

Geneva, Partition, and the "Temporary" Line

Following the French defeat at Điện Biên Phủ in May 1954, the Geneva Conference produced a set of agreements — known collectively as the Geneva Accords — signed on July 21, 1954. The accords established a cease-fire line at the 17th parallel, dividing Vietnam into a northern zone under Việt Minh (Hồ Chí Minh's) control and a southern zone under the State of Vietnam, headed nominally by former emperor Bảo Đại. Both sides were given 300 days to withdraw their forces to their respective sides of the line.

Crucially, the Geneva documents explicitly stated that the partition line "should not in any way be interpreted as constituting a political or territorial boundary" — the division was meant to be temporary, pending nationwide elections scheduled for 1956 that would reunify the country under a single government.

Those elections never took place. The State of Vietnam in the South had not signed the Geneva Accords and did not consider itself bound by them. Ngô Đình Diệm, who became Prime Minister of the State of Vietnam in 1954 and pushed Bảo Đại aside the following year, refused to participate in the 1956 elections, arguing that free elections were impossible in the communist-controlled North and that the South was under no obligation to hold them. The United States supported this position, having also not signed the Geneva Accords.

The partition was accompanied by a large population movement: roughly one million people, many of them Catholics and others wary of communist rule, moved from North to South in an operation that came to be known as "Operation Passage to Freedom," assisted by French and American naval and air transport.

  • May 7, 1954 Fall of the French garrison at Điện Biên Phủ.
  • July 21, 1954 Geneva Accords signed, establishing the 17th parallel cease-fire line and a 1956 deadline for reunification elections.
  • 1954–1955 "Operation Passage to Freedom" relocates roughly one million people from North to South Vietnam.
  • 1955 Ngô Đình Diệm proclaims the Republic of Vietnam, refuses to hold the 1956 reunification elections.
Point of dispute: Whether the South's refusal to hold the 1956 elections was a justified response to the impossibility of free elections under a communist regime in the North, or a unilateral abrogation of an international agreement that locked in the division of the country, remains one of the most contested questions in the historiography of the war's origins. Both framings appear regularly in mainstream accounts.
1955 – 1963

The Diệm Years and the Rise of the Insurgency

Ngô Đình Diệm consolidated power in the South through the mid-1950s, suppressing rival political and religious factions and securing growing American support as a bulwark against communism in Southeast Asia. However, his government's land policies, favoritism toward the Catholic minority in a majority-Buddhist country, and increasingly authoritarian rule generated mounting opposition.

Beginning around 1959, North Vietnam's ruling Lao Động (Workers') Party formally linked the consolidation of socialism in the North with the goal of reunifying the country, and began actively supporting an armed insurgency in the South. The guerrilla fighters — many of them veterans of the earlier Việt Minh — became known to their opponents as the "Viet Cong" (a contraction of "Việt Nam Cộng Sản," or Vietnamese Communist). By the early 1960s, this insurgency had grown into a serious security challenge for the Diệm government across large parts of rural South Vietnam.

Diệm's government collapsed in November 1963, when he was overthrown and killed in a military coup that had at least tacit American acquiescence. The coup ushered in a period of political instability in Saigon, with a succession of short-lived military governments over the following years — instability that occurred just weeks before U.S. President John F. Kennedy was himself assassinated.

August 1964

The Gulf of Tonkin Incident and U.S. Escalation

On August 2, 1964, the U.S. destroyer USS Maddox, conducting a signals-intelligence patrol in the Gulf of Tonkin, was approached and fired upon by North Vietnamese torpedo boats; the Maddox returned fire, damaging the boats and killing several North Vietnamese sailors, with no American casualties. Two days later, on August 4, the Maddox and a second destroyer, the USS Turner Joy, reported a second attack based on radar contacts and intercepted communications.

The second incident has been the subject of long-running historical controversy, with subsequent research — including the U.S. Navy's own later historical reviews — raising significant doubt about whether an attack actually occurred on August 4, or whether the reported radar and sonar contacts were the result of weather conditions and equipment errors.

Regardless of what happened on the water, the political consequences were immediate and far-reaching. On August 5, Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, authorizing President Lyndon B. Johnson to use military force in Southeast Asia without a formal declaration of war. This resolution became the legal basis for the massive escalation of American involvement that followed over the next year, including sustained bombing campaigns against North Vietnam and the deployment of U.S. ground combat troops.

  • August 2, 1964 USS Maddox engages North Vietnamese torpedo boats in the Gulf of Tonkin.
  • August 4, 1964 A second, disputed incident is reported by U.S. destroyers.
  • August 5, 1964 Congress passes the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution.
  • 1965 U.S. begins sustained bombing of North Vietnam (Operation Rolling Thunder) and deploys ground combat troops to South Vietnam.
1965 – 1967

Americanization of the War

From 1965 onward, the conflict shifted decisively from a primarily South Vietnamese counterinsurgency to a war in which American forces took the leading combat role. U.S. troop levels rose rapidly, supported by South Korean, Australian, Thai, Filipino, and New Zealand contingents, while South Vietnam's own armed forces (the ARVN) were expanded substantially. By 1968, U.S. troop strength alone exceeded half a million.

The American strategy combined large-scale bombing campaigns against the North and the Hồ Chí Minh Trail (the supply network running through Laos and Cambodia) with "search and destroy" ground operations aimed at attriting North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces in the South. By early 1968, U.S. military leadership expressed considerable optimism that this strategy was steadily degrading the enemy's capacity to fight.

January – March 1968

The Tet Offensive: A Military Defeat, A Political Turning Point

On January 31, 1968 — the first day of the Lunar New Year (Tết) — North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces launched a massive, coordinated surprise offensive against cities and military installations across South Vietnam, including Saigon itself and the former imperial capital of Huế. The scale and coordination of the attacks stunned American and South Vietnamese commanders, who had been told the enemy was being steadily weakened.

Militarily, the offensive was a severe defeat for North Vietnam and the Viet Cong. Communist forces seized numerous towns and cities in the initial days but were unable to hold most of them for more than a few days under the weight of American and ARVN counterattacks; the Viet Cong's underground infrastructure in the South was particularly badly damaged, with estimates of at least 40,000 to 60,000 communist fighters killed during the main offensive. American and South Vietnamese losses, while far smaller in absolute numbers, were also significant — roughly 1,000 U.S. and 2,000 ARVN personnel killed in the initial weeks, according to figures commonly cited in historical accounts, with total Tet-period casualties on all sides considerably higher when later phases of the offensive through 1968 are included.

Politically and in terms of public perception, however, Tet was a turning point against the American war effort. Images of fighting reaching the U.S. embassy compound in Saigon and the destruction of Huế contradicted the optimistic assessments American officials had been giving, and contributed to a sharp decline in U.S. public support for the war. In the aftermath, President Johnson announced he would not seek re-election, and the U.S. began a gradual shift toward "Vietnamization" — transferring more of the combat burden to South Vietnamese forces.

1969 – 1973

Vietnamization, Secret Negotiations, and the Paris Peace Accords

Under President Nixon, the U.S. pursued a dual-track approach: gradually withdrawing American ground troops while expanding South Vietnamese forces ("Vietnamization"), even as the war's ground and air operations extended into neighboring Cambodia and Laos in an effort to disrupt North Vietnamese supply lines and sanctuaries — moves that proved highly controversial both internationally and within the United States.

Simultaneously, secret negotiations between U.S. National Security Adviser Henry Kissinger and North Vietnamese representative Lê Đức Thọ had been underway since 1969. A near-agreement in October 1972 was delayed by objections from South Vietnamese President Nguyễn Văn Thiệu, who feared the terms would leave his government dangerously exposed. After a final, intense round of American bombing of North Vietnam in December 1972 (the "Christmas Bombing"), the Paris Peace Accords were signed on January 27, 1973.

The accords provided for a cease-fire, the withdrawal of remaining U.S. forces (completed by March 29, 1973), the return of American prisoners of war, and a framework — never implemented — for eventual political reconciliation between North and South Vietnam. Both the Joint Military Commission and an International Commission of Control and Supervision (with representatives from Poland, Hungary, Canada, and Indonesia) were established to oversee compliance, but violations of the cease-fire by both sides began almost immediately.

  • 1969 Secret Paris talks begin between Kissinger and Lê Đức Thọ; U.S. ground operations expand into Cambodia.
  • October 1972 A draft agreement is nearly finalized but delayed by South Vietnamese objections.
  • December 1972 U.S. conducts intensive bombing of North Vietnam ("Christmas Bombing") to pressure both Hanoi and Saigon toward agreement.
  • January 27, 1973 Paris Peace Accords signed; cease-fire takes effect.
  • March 29, 1973 Last U.S. combat troops withdraw from Vietnam.
1973 – 1975

The Final Offensive and the Fall of Saigon

After the U.S. withdrawal, fighting between North and South Vietnamese forces continued despite the cease-fire terms. With American combat troops gone — and U.S. domestic politics increasingly consumed by the Watergate scandal, which limited Congress's appetite for renewed intervention — North Vietnam began preparing for a final offensive.

That offensive, launched in early 1975, advanced far more quickly than even North Vietnamese planners had anticipated. South Vietnamese defenses collapsed in the Central Highlands and along the coast through March and April 1975. On April 30, 1975, North Vietnamese forces entered Saigon, and the government of South Vietnam surrendered, ending the war.

The scale and speed of the collapse, and the chaotic American evacuation of Saigon in its final days — including the now-iconic images of helicopters being pushed off the decks of U.S. Navy ships to make room for evacuees — became defining images of the war's end, and of the broader question of what the United States' commitments to its allies had ultimately been worth.

  • 1973–1974 Cease-fire violations continue; U.S. military and economic aid to South Vietnam declines sharply.
  • March 1975 North Vietnam's final offensive (the "Spring Offensive" / "Hồ Chí Minh Campaign") advances rapidly through the Central Highlands and central coast.
  • April 30, 1975 Fall of Saigon; South Vietnam surrenders unconditionally, ending the Vietnam War.
